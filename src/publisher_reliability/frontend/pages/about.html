About this tool

Publisher Reliability Tool

An open-source workspace for inspecting how a language model rates the reliability of a news publisher, one article at a time.

What it does

A reliability study normally arrives as a table of aggregate scores, and there is no way to ask why one publisher ended up where it did. This tool turns such a study back into something you can open.

  • Classify one article. Paste a public article URL and a five-class encoder runs on it, on CPU, in a couple of seconds.
  • Read a publisher's class. It is not stored anywhere: it is computed, when you ask for it, from the articles of that publisher already classified. Six documented formulas are offered, and they do not always agree.
  • Follow the provenance. Every prediction carries all five class probabilities, the exact checkpoint that produced it, and the run it came from.

The study behind it

The predictions and the method come from From Articles to Publishers: Aggregating Language Model Predictions for News Source Reliability Inference, by John Bianchi, Manuel Pratelli, Fabio Pinelli and Marinella Petrocchi — IEEE Transactions on Computational Social Systems, 2026, under review. The preprint is on arXiv.

This application is the reference implementation of that study, and is itself the subject of a demonstration paper submitted to ECIR 2027.

What is in the release

34,564 article-level predictions over 17,269 English political news articles from 372 publishers, produced by BERT and RoBERTa classifiers under a five-fold, publisher-disjoint protocol. Each article was classified by the fold that was not trained on it.

Class 0 to Class 4 are reliability bands in ascending order, the lowest covering the least reliable sources. Training labels came from NewsGuard's published ratings; those ratings are licensed material and are not part of this release. What ships is the model output derived from them.

What it is not

Every class on every page is a model prediction. It is not a fact check, not a verdict on an article's contents, and not a ground-truth rating of a publisher. The tool carries no reference labels, so nothing here can tell you whether a prediction is correct — only how the articles behind it agree or disagree.

Nothing in the tool deletes anything. There is no button and no route that removes a prediction, a saved article or a job record.
